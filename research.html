Job market paper

How Shareholder Voice Shapes Board Agendas

with Nadya Malenko (Boston College) and Ronald W. Masulis (University of New South Wales)

Abstract

Board voting offers an incomplete picture of director influence, overlooking board agenda-setting and board–shareholder meeting interactions. Using unique corporate disclosures in China, we examine the full decision process—from board agenda formation to shareholder voting. Minority blockholder-affiliated directors connect board decisions to shareholder voting by bringing minority investors’ voting power into board deliberations. Exploiting a policy shock that increases visibility of dissent at shareholder meetings, we find that stronger shareholder voice amplifies these directors’ influence on agenda setting: tunneling proposals are less likely to reach board agendas, especially when they require shareholder approval. The effect also extends to proposals not requiring shareholder approval, consistent with minority blockholders using opposition to other shareholder-meeting proposals as a bargaining lever.

Other working papers

Monitoring Without Dissent: Director Communication Before Board Meetings

with Nadya Malenko (Boston College) and Ronald W. Masulis (University of New South Wales)

Abstract

Unanimous board votes can reflect active monitoring before the meeting, rather than weak oversight. Using unique Chinese board disclosures, we find that greater opportunities for pre-vote director communication are associated with fewer board proposals raising agency concerns. Vote delegation following personal shocks to attendance captures pre-vote director contact and shows that pre-vote coordination among independent directors and bargaining with insiders are associated with fewer agency-concerning proposals on the board agenda. Director absence and remote participation, which do not require pre-vote director contact, show no significant reduction in such proposals. Exploiting a policy reform that expands pre-vote communication among independent directors, we find that broader communication improves firm value, especially when the committee that screens problematic proposals before the reform is not independent.

Post-Voice Share Sales and Purchases: Analysis of Blockholder Strategic Behavior after their Directors Vote against a Board Proposal

with Ronald W. Masulis (University of New South Wales)

Abstract

By exploiting the public disclosure of Chinese board proposals and director voting, we find that minority shareholders sell more shares after their appointed directors dissent in boardroom voting, while the largest shareholder is more apt to buy shares. Smaller minority shareholders sell more shares than larger minority shareholders. Minority-shareholder-appointed directors’ dissents on tunneling-related proposals trigger negative share price impacts, and the dissenting minority shareholders subsequently do not significantly sell or buy shares. However, the price impacts are statistically insignificant for disclosure-related dissent from minority-shareholder-appointed directors, and the dissenting minority shareholders subsequently sell more shares. These patterns suggest that the price impact of voice influences whether ‘voice’ and ‘exit’ are substitutes or complements. ‘Voice’ and ‘exit’ act as substitutes for dissent on tunneling-related but act as complements for disclosure-related proposals. The phenomenon that smaller minority shareholders sell more than larger minority shareholders is mainly found in disclosure-related dissenting votes. Finally, when the largest-shareholder-appointed directors decide to dissent on more disclosure-related proposals than tunneling-related ones, the ex-ante largest shareholding is smaller. However, if the largest shareholding is not too small and buying for control is not too costly, then the dissenting largest shareholder often buys more shares afterward.

Mandated Green, Masked Tunneling: Stakeholder Pressure-Induced Shareholder Expropriation

with Cong Wang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen)

Abstract

We find that pressure on firms to advance stakeholder interests can intensify conflicts between controlling and minority shareholders. Using unique data on board agendas in China, we exploit the staggered rollout of central environmental inspection policies to study private firms’ responses to public environmental pressure. Greater public environmental pressure shifts board agendas toward green projects but with greater tunneling concerns (related-party transactions), without a corresponding increase in arm’s-length green projects. Directors representing large minority blockholders constrain the initiation of these green tunneling projects. However, these projects’ green label weakens regulatory and shareholder scrutiny towards tunneling. Our findings suggest that controllers can exploit the public legitimacy of green projects while shifting the associated green costs to minority shareholders through tunneling.

Public Policy, Polarized Shareholders

with Yiyang Zou (New York University) and Jianhong Long (University of New South Wales)

Abstract

Social policy can encourage pro-social shareholders’ activism while leaving other shareholders polarized. We study the staggered expansion of Medicaid across U.S. states to examine how socially-oriented state policies shape shareholder engagement through socially-oriented shareholder proposals. Firms headquartered in expansion states reflect a shift toward more pro-social norms in those states and receive more pro-social proposals from shareholders. However, these socially oriented proposals become more likely to be withdrawn and receive less support when they reach a vote, reflecting disapproval from other shareholders. Our findings suggest that public enforcement of socially oriented policies can intensify polarization among shareholders in their attitudes toward firms’ social responsibilities.

There Is No Such Thing as Bad Publicity: Corporate Scandal Experience and Entry-Level Job Interviews

with Bohui Zhang (The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen) and Kemin Wang (Fudan University)

Abstract

Corporate scandals can damage a firm’s reputation while increasing its visibility in the competition for attention. Using a randomized field experiment in China, we examine how these competing effects shape the interview prospects of entry-level candidates with experience at scandal-tainted firms when they apply for new jobs. Fictitious résumés listing experience at scandal-tainted firms receive interview callbacks at a rate of 10.5%, compared with 6% for applications listing experience at firms without scandals, representing a 75% increase. Rational factors such as labor-market conditions, the characteristics of recruiting firms and former employers, and applicants’ other characteristics do not explain the result. Public attention to scandals, measured by Baidu search volume, explains substantial variation in callback rates. The findings suggest that the benefits of attention can outweigh concerns associated with a former employer’s misconduct, which reveals how corporate visibility can benefit former employees when they compete for attention in the job market.
